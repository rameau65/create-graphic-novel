# Story and continuity

## Adaptation hierarchy

1. Preserve the source thesis and ending implication.
2. Preserve causal order where it matters.
3. Compress repetition before removing distinct ideas.
4. Replace abstract explanation with visual metaphor only when the metaphor cannot reverse the meaning.
5. Use narration for what cannot be shown economically.

## Beat design

Use a compact arc suited to the requested length:

`hook → orientation → pressure/question → turn → insight/climax → integration/afterimage`

A cover is outside this count unless the user says otherwise. Each story page needs one sentence answering, “What changes here?” If nothing changes, merge it with another page or make its stillness deliberately meaningful.

## Character bible

For every recurring character, lock:

- name and story function
- age range and apparent ethnicity when relevant
- face geometry, skin tone, eyes, hair, body and height
- costume layers, colors, footwear, accessories
- signature pose, emotional baseline, and distinctive silhouette
- owned props and which hand carries them
- forbidden drift: hairstyle, age, clothing, scale, missing/extra accessories

Differentiate similar characters with at least three visible cues. In multi-character panels, specify position, posture, direction, and action for each person.

## Continuity ledger

Track per page or cut:

`character state | costume | prop state/count | injuries/marks | screen position | gaze | geography | time | weather | light direction | motif state`

Establish left/right positions in a wide anchor shot. Maintain them in close-ups through matching gaze direction. Mark an intentional axis break and its emotional purpose.

## Text economy

- Prefer one idea per balloon or caption.
- Aim for no more than three text units in an ordinary panel.
- Let images carry action and emotion; do not narrate what the reader can already see.
- Preserve breathing space after dense or philosophical lines.
- Keep exact quotations exact and distinguish them from adaptation.
